# Numerical methods and reproduction details

This document gives the implementation settings behind the manuscript's finite-cutoff calculations, SNAP-plus-displacement (SNAP+D) fits, and Gaussian-circuit error estimates. The [README](README.md) maps the results to their code and data and gives the environment and commands for bounded reproduction runs.

## Finite representations and propagation

Calculations use double-precision dense or sparse matrices. States at different Fock cutoffs are zero-padded into a common basis before comparison. Coherent inputs are projected onto each cutoff and normalized. Their first moments set the initial conditions for the classical trajectories.

For Duffing, [`hard_sandwiched_operators`](duffing_code/duffing_padded_hybrid_validation.py) constructs the quartic in dimension $N+4$ and then projects onto the first $N$ levels. This gives the matrix of $P_N H_{\rm D}P_N$, including paths through intermediate Fock levels outside the output space. The lattice calculations instead use matrix functions of the truncated quadratures. In particular, the cosine is obtained by diagonalizing the truncated phase operator. The lattice operators and propagation routines are in [`sglib.py`](sine_gordon_code/sglib.py) and [`run_pilot.py`](common_code/run_pilot.py).

Duffing trajectories use diagonalization at 401 observation times. The sine-Gordon mean-field comparison uses a Strang propagator with 1024 steps, checked against 2048 steps, and 257 observation times. The double sine-Gordon target uses sparse matrix-exponential propagation at 257 observation times. The independent Schrödinger-equation integration uses SciPy `solve_ivp` with method `DOP853`, relative tolerance `2e-11`, and absolute tolerance `2e-13`. Classical trajectories use the same method with relative tolerance `2e-10` and absolute tolerance `2e-12`. The cutoff-160 target check is implemented in [`run_sg_padding160.py`](double_sine_gordon_code/run_sg_padding160.py). The separate [quadratic-grounding check](double_sine_gordon_code/dsg_grounding_check.py) uses relative tolerance `1e-11` and absolute tolerance `1e-13` for its independent target integration.

For the exact-block splitting comparisons, potential and correction blocks are diagonal phases in the eigenbasis of the phase quadrature. Native, kinetic, and Gaussian blocks use sparse matrix-exponential propagation. [`splitting_baselines.py`](double_sine_gordon_code/splitting_baselines.py) and [`splitting_sweep.py`](double_sine_gordon_code/splitting_sweep.py) compare the split trajectory with the target at the same cutoff.

The three-site calculation in [`dsg_chain.py`](double_sine_gordon_code/dsg_chain.py) uses adaptive Lanczos propagation with full reorthogonalization for the target and native intervals. Its two ancillary-qubit components are propagated separately. The tolerance is `1e-11` per propagation. A direct propagation to the final time at tolerance `1e-13` changes the state by $3\times10^{-8}$, which supplies the empirical solver margin. Reference, solver, and padding margins are added to the measured state distance. The manuscript gives the cutoff pairs and the state, observable, and boundary-population acceptance criteria.

## SNAP+D optimization

The compiler fits the action of a block on training columns with one common phase and a displacement penalty of $10^{-8}\operatorname{mean}_\ell|\alpha_\ell|^2$. Gradients use the Fréchet derivative of the finite matrix exponential. SciPy SLSQP uses `ftol=1e-12`, SNAP phases in $[-\pi,\pi]$, and the real and imaginary parts of each displacement in $[-1.5,1.5]$. The objective, analytic gradient, parameter bounds, and stopping conditions are implemented in [`duffing_padded_hybrid_validation.py`](duffing_code/duffing_padded_hybrid_validation.py).

### Duffing training coverage

The real-amplitude search uses initial coherent amplitudes 1, 1.5, and 2.25, with outer-step counts $r\in\{10,20\}$ and layer counts $L\in\{4,8,12\}$. Each configuration has three starts and an allowance of 1200 iterations. One circuit per configuration is selected by its training objective. These six circuits reproduce the two real benchmark inputs with amplitude errors at most 0.031, but their maximum distances across the ten development inputs range from 0.85 to 1.38. The largest error occurs at $|2i\rangle$. The [formal runs](duffing_data/formal/E2/) store the fits and their evaluation records.

The eight-phase fit uses 24 coherent initial states with amplitudes $\rho e^{im\pi/4}$, where $\rho\in\{1,1.5,2.25\}$ and $m=0,\ldots,7$. It uses $r=10$, $L=12$, 48 SNAP levels, padding 96, and reference cutoff 80. Training columns are the states entering the quartic block along the exact Duffing trajectory. The refinement uses three starts with an allowance of 5000 iterations per start. The selected training objective is $8.0\times10^{-6}$. The [refinement driver](duffing_code/run_duffing_refinement.py), [protocol](duffing_data/manifests/duffing_refinement_protocol.json), and [records](duffing_data/duffing_refinement/) specify the random seeds, time limits, stopping information, and selected parameters.

In `paired_frame_parameters`, NumPy's PCG64 generator draws displacement radii uniformly from $[0.3,0.8]$ and phases uniformly from $[0,2\pi)$. Adjacent displacement parameters are opposite pairs. The first SNAP layer uses the phases of the target block's diagonal entries, and the remaining SNAP layers start at zero. The initial circuit is number diagonal, while independent changes to the paired displacements can change its action away from that diagonal circuit.

Selection uses the training objective before evaluation on twelve additional coherent inputs. Their amplitudes are drawn uniformly from $[1,2.25]$ and phases uniformly from $[0,2\pi)$ with a fixed seed. The realized inputs and seed are saved in [`duffing_refinement_inputs.json`](duffing_data/manifests/duffing_refinement_inputs.json). Together with ten development inputs, they give the 22-state evaluation set. The comparison between real-amplitude training and the eight-phase fit also changes initialization and the optimization budget, so its outcome measures the combined training procedure.

### Double sine-Gordon correction searches

The local target is $e^{-ih\lambda_2(1-\cos2\phi)}$. Training uses the lowest Fock states, and the fitted local circuit is applied to both sites between native half steps. The two-site evaluation uses $E_J=\kappa=1$, $\lambda_2=0.3$, $\omega_b=\sqrt3$, and $T=5.2/\sqrt3$, with the product coherent preparation specified by first moments $\langle\boldsymbol\phi\rangle=(1,0)$ and $\langle\boldsymbol\Pi\rangle=(0,0)$.

## Gaussian circuits at finite cutoff

The counted Gaussian circuit has two boundary half intervals and full interior Gaussian intervals at each readout. Commuting position phases merge by adding their quadratic generators. Truncated quadratures do not satisfy the canonical commutation relation, so replacing two finite half-interval shear products with one full-interval product changes the finite circuit. Its error must be evaluated for the sequence whose primitives are counted.

The original scan files describe the repeated-half-interval sequence. The comparison records measure its separation from the merged sequence at both cutoffs. At readout $j$, let $b_j$ be the repeated sequence's Bures distance from the target and $\delta_j$ the projective distance between the two joint oscillator–qubit outputs at the lower cutoff. Contractivity under the qubit trace and the Bures triangle inequality give the following interval for the merged circuit's maximum sampled state error after adding numerical margins:

$$
\max_j\max(0,b_j-\delta_j)+m_{\rm num}\le e_B\le\max_j(b_j+\delta_j)+m_{\rm num}.
$$

Here $m_{\rm num}$ is the sum of reference and solver margins and the padding difference measured for the merged circuit. The margins are empirical estimates. The triangle inequality bounds the finite-circuit separation independently of how those margins estimate the remaining numerical error. When only maxima are used, $b_{\max}=\max_j b_j$ and $\delta_{\max}=\max_j\delta_j$ give the coarser interval

$$
\max(0,b_{\max}-\delta_{\max})+m_{\rm num}\le e_B\le b_{\max}+\delta_{\max}+m_{\rm num}.
$$

Observable-error intervals follow from the measured changes in $C_{12}$ and $S_2$ between the two sequences. Observable cutoff changes and boundary populations are evaluated for the merged circuit. The paper's Gaussian-block state errors use the upper estimates.

[`gaussian_evidence.py`](double_sine_gordon_code/gaussian_evidence.py) matches candidates by system size, harmonic strength, preparation, cutoffs, outer-step count, block orders, and subdivision count. It combines the original scan rows with [`gaussian_revision`](common_data/records/gaussian_revision/) comparison records using [`gaussian_revision_candidates.json`](common_data/manifests/gaussian_revision_candidates.json). The output preserves upper estimates in the scalar error fields and supplies lower bounds and an evidence label. Hybrid and kinetic–potential rows use their direct trajectory results.

For a selected amplitude-distance threshold $\epsilon$, the boundary and observable criteria must pass and the reference and solver margins must each be at most $\epsilon/10$. A state-error upper estimate within the threshold establishes acceptance. A lower estimate above it establishes failure. A threshold lying inside an unresolved interval stops selection with an error. Any additional implementation-error allowance is included before this state-error decision. The [README's Gaussian circuit instructions](README.md#gaussian-circuit-evidence) give the bounded comparison manifests and replay commands.

## Joint Fourier phase compilation

[`joint_phase.py`](double_sine_gordon_code/joint_phase.py) constructs the grouped Fourier response and its unitary qubit completion. For Fourier truncation $P_{M_F}$ and tail bound $\epsilon_{\rm tail}$, the numerical normalization is $F=P_{M_F}/(1+\epsilon_{\rm tail}+10^{-13})$. The last term supplies roundoff slack. Spectral-factor roots are chosen inside the unit circle. The manuscript states the exact-arithmetic tail bounds and the effect of the completion phase on repeated blocks.

The recovered response factors into balanced controlled displacements and constant qubit rotations. Constant rotations are combined across intervening system-only evolution, decomposed into $R_zR_yR_z$, and simplified by merging commuting $R_z$ rotations. Alternating completion signs implement $Q\mapsto-Q$ through ancillary-qubit $Z$ conjugation, absorbed into the constant rotations before counting.

[`check_joint_phase.py`](double_sine_gordon_code/check_joint_phase.py) constructs 120 compiler configurations for two sites at $\lambda_2=0.3$, $r=6,7,8$, degrees $M_F=1,\ldots,10$, and fixed or alternating completion. It evaluates ten full circuits at cutoffs 128/160. Eight are the Gaussian-block candidates below 209 primitives, and two are representative accurate circuits. Their results and pulse counts are saved in [`joint_phase_check.json`](double_sine_gordon_data/joint_phase_check.json). The single-block error estimate combines a sampled maximum over the signal circle with a second-derivative interpolation allowance. The [README](README.md) gives the bounded replay command and its resource limits.

## Control times from pulse actions

[`dsg_action_time.py`](double_sine_gordon_code/dsg_action_time.py) evaluates serial control times for the 67 primary-preparation TGIFS circuits in the two-site step scan at $E_J=\kappa=1$, $\lambda_2=0.3$, and $s=1$. It uses hybrid, Gaussian-block, and kinetic–potential routes with the existing merged-circuit evidence and numerical acceptance rules. The additional state-distance allowances are 0, 0.005, 0.010, and 0.020. The calculation reads stored errors and constructs pulse schedules without propagating states or fitting gates.

For each pulse $\exp(-i\gamma G)$, its time is a per-call overhead plus $|\gamma|$ times the inverse control rate. Controlled displacements use their displacement magnitude, and qubit rotations use their full rotation angle. The quadratic primitives are normalized using $X_j=\sqrt{\omega_b}\phi_j$ and $P_j=\Pi_j/\sqrt{\omega_b}$ with $\omega_b=\sqrt3$. Their generators are $X_j^2$, $P_j^2$, $X_1X_2$, and $P_1P_2$, without an implicit factor of one half. Counts and absolute coefficient sums are saved separately for all seven primitive types. Allowed pulse merges add signed coefficients before taking absolute values. The displayed slices assume equal rates for positive and negative coefficients and charge $R_z$ as a physical rotation. Changing the quadrature or generator normalization requires changing the associated rates.

The trigonometric schedules reuse [`trig_counts.py`](double_sine_gordon_code/trig_counts.py). Only the initial and final qubit basis changes remain after cancellation across system intervals. Controlled displacements on opposite sides of a system interval are not merged. For the hybrid routes with first- and second-order blocks, the displacement actions are $16ra_1$ and $24ra_1$, where $a_1=1/(2\sqrt{2\omega_b})$. Both have rotation action $A_y=\pi+4T\lambda_2$. The programmable mixed-order route has displacement action $28ra_1$ and rotation action $A_y=\pi+4T(E_J+\lambda_2)$. All these routes have $A_z=\pi$. These identities include both sites and the boundary basis rotations.

The Gaussian-block intervals have durations $h/2,h,\ldots,h,h/2$. Each interval uses the shear coefficients in Appendix F of the manuscript. Adjacent position shears merge by adding coefficients, giving $r+2$ position factors and $r+1$ momentum factors. In the normalized basis the total position action is $A_{X^2}=A_{XX}=2\sum_i a(\tau_i)/\omega_b$, with $a(\tau)=\sqrt{2\kappa}\tan(\sqrt{2\kappa}\tau/2)/2$. The momentum actions obey $A_{P^2}+A_{PP}=\omega_b T$. For the kinetic–potential route, $A_{X^2}=A_{XX}=\kappa T/\omega_b$ and $A_{P^2}=\omega_b T$. The Gaussian-block total quadratic action exceeds the kinetic–potential value and approaches it as $r$ increases on the shear branch connected to zero. This comparison uses the fixed normalization and equal inverse rates for the four quadratic generators.

The plotted slices use a time unit $\tau_A$ per unit displacement or rotation angle, a common per-call overhead $u_0\tau_A$, and time $u_G\tau_A$ per unit quadratic coefficient. Thus the programmable time is $p_c/\tau_A=u_0N_c+A_{\rm CD}+A_y+A_z+u_GA_G$. The stored sensitivity study also varies the local and cross-mode quadratic rates separately. These are control-cost parameters, not measured device rates. The model assumes serial execution and effective control of native drift during programmable pulses. It does not simulate pulse-shaping errors or duration-dependent noise.

For every cost setting, each route selects its cheapest candidate satisfying the unchanged numerical criteria and $e_B+\epsilon_{\rm add}\le\sqrt{10^{-3}}$. This inequality reserves an implementation-error budget without estimating physical errors. The fastest programmable candidate is selected across both programmable routes. Native intervals add $(r+1)\tau_{\rm pair}+t_{\rm nat}$, and the difference between the programmable minimum and the hybrid minimum excluding $t_{\rm nat}$ gives the break-even native duration. A nonpositive difference gives no faster hybrid circuit for a nonnegative native duration. Selection stops with an explicit error if a route has no accepted candidate. Rankings apply to the evaluated TGIFS step-scan candidates. Joint Fourier and QSP circuits are outside this timing pool.

[`dsg_action_time.json`](double_sine_gordon_data/dsg_action_time.json) records the input hashes, environment, candidates, actions, eligibility, and selections. The two figures are `paper/figures/fig_dsg_action_time.pdf` and `paper/figures/fig_dsg_action_components.pdf`. The second figure shows algebraic action formulas as functions of step count and does not assert trajectory acceptance at every plotted count. [`test_action_time.py`](double_sine_gordon_code/test_action_time.py) checks the action identities, canonical symplectic shear map, quadratic-action limit, cost domains, and recovery of the fixed-count comparison on the same hybrid and Gaussian-block candidate pools.
